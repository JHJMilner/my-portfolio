100 Days of Drawing Badly

What happens when a researcher can't resist running a study on her own sketchbook. One small drawing a day, for one hundred days — most of them genuinely bad, all of them worth it.

Role
Chief Doodler & Test Subject
Format
The 100 Day Project
Timeline
100 days (a few were definitely skipped)
Kit
Cheap fineliners and a £4 sketchbook
Subjects
Mostly the cat. A surprising number of chairs.
Final Tally
87 of 100 (we don't discuss days 41–53)
The Premise

A hobby with absolutely no deliverable

The 100 Day Project is a simple, slightly terrifying idea: pick one creative thing and do it every day for a hundred days, then share it. No prizes, no client, no point — which, for someone whose day job is producing useful outcomes for other people, was exactly the appeal.

I picked drawing because I'm bad at it. The blank page makes me anxious in a way that spreadsheets never will, and I wanted to see what would happen if I sat in that discomfort daily instead of avoiding it. Mostly I wanted something that was just for me.

The Rules

One rule, ruthlessly enforced

The only real rule was show up. Everything else existed to lower the bar far enough that I couldn't talk myself out of it:

  • Fifteen minutes, maximum. A timer, not a masterpiece.
  • No erasing. The wonky line stays in.
  • Post it the same day, however ugly.
  • A missed day is a missed day — no doubling up to "catch up".
The Method

I couldn't help myself: it became a study

Old habits. Within a week I'd turned a gentle creative practice into a tiny longitudinal study, because apparently I cannot do a hobby without instrumenting it.

  • Same slot every day — first coffee, before email could ruin my mood.
  • A one-word feeling logged next to each drawing ("smug", "rushed", "grumpy").
  • Every page photographed, so I had a clean dataset to look back on.
  • A simple tally of subjects, to see what I reached for when I had no idea what to draw.
What I Found

The bad drawings did the heavy lifting

  • Consistency beat inspiration, every time. The "I don't feel like it" days produced the work I learned most from.
  • Lowering the stakes raised the output. Fifteen ugly minutes is easy to start; a "good drawing" is not.
  • Sharing the worst ones was strangely freeing — and got the warmest responses.
  • My mood log and my drawings barely correlated. Grumpy days made perfectly fine pears.
What It Changed

A looser hand, and a useful reminder

  • 87 finished drawings, up from a lifelong total of roughly zero.
  • A daily ritual that survived the project and is still going.
  • A much looser, less precious line — the erasing rule did its job.
  • The kicker, for the day job: a fresh, first-hand reminder of how it feels to be a beginner on display. I ask research participants to fumble through unfamiliar tasks while I watch. A hundred days of public bad drawing rebuilt my empathy for exactly that discomfort.
What's Next

Round two, with worse subjects

I'm tempted to run it back — same fifteen-minute rule, but drawing only things I find genuinely hard. Hands. Bicycles. The cat, in motion, refusing to cooperate.

If you've been circling a 100 Day Project of your own: the secret is that it isn't about the drawings. It's about proving to yourself that you'll show up. Pick the thing you're a bit scared of, set a timer, and let the first few be terrible. They're supposed to be.

Turns out the point was never the drawings. It was showing up to be bad at something, on purpose, in public.
